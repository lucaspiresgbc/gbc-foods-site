An offer for Brazilian green coffee usually reads like a code: *NY 2/3, screen 17/18, Fine Cup, natural*. Each part answers a different question — how big the bean is, how it tastes, and how clean the lot is — and each one moves the price. Here is how to read them.

## Screen: the size of the bean

Beans are sorted through metal screens with round holes measured in 64ths of an inch. Screen 17 has holes of 17/64″, about 6.75 mm; screen 18, 18/64″, about 7.14 mm. A lot sold as **17/18** is made of beans retained on the 17 and 18 screens, that is, the larger beans of the crop. **16/18** widens the range downwards; **14/16** is the size band of the commercial grades.

Size matters for two reasons. Larger beans tend to come from higher, slower-ripening plots, so screen and altitude travel together — which is why our Special Coffee 17/18 comes from the 1,000 to 1,250 metre slopes and the Good Cup 14/16 from the valley floors. And a uniform size roasts evenly: a lot with a tight screen band gives the roaster fewer surprises than a wide mix of sizes.

## Cup: how it tastes

Brazil's official classification (COB) grades the cup from *strictly soft* through *soft*, *softish* and *hard* down to *Rioy* and *Rio*, the last two marking the iodine-like taste of fermentation gone wrong. The export trade compresses that scale into two names you will see on most offers:

- **Fine Cup** — a clean cup, soft to strictly soft, no harshness. This is the specialty and premium-commercial band.
- **Good Cup** — a clean cup with some hardness; sound coffee for blends and for markets that roast dark.

Above both sits **Special Coffee**, used for the lots that a cupper scores highest — in our case, the sun-dried naturals from the higher slopes, offered in limited lots each harvest.

## Defects: how clean the lot is

The COB counts defects in a 300-gram sample — black beans, sours, shells, sticks, stones, husks — and turns the count into a type: type 2 has 4 defects, type 3 has 12, type 4 has 26, and so on down to type 8. The New York contract convention **NY 2/3** means a lot between types 2 and 3, roughly 8 defects per 300 g, which is the standard for good export coffee. A lot offered as NY 4/5 is a cheaper, less clean coffee, and the price reflects it.

## Process: how it was dried

Most Brazilian coffee is **natural**: the whole cherry is dried in the sun, on a patio or in a dryer, and the fruit sugars stay with the bean during drying. That is the origin of the chocolate, caramel and dried-fruit sweetness in a good Brazilian cup, and of its heavier body. Pulped naturals and washed lots exist, but natural is the house style of southern Minas Gerais and the only process in our current offer.

## Putting it together

When you write a specification, the three numbers do most of the work: *screen 16/18, Fine Cup, NY 2/3, natural* tells us the size, the cup and the cleanliness you need, and lets us pull the right lots from the cooperative. Add moisture — the export norm is around 11 to 12 % — the packing, the volume and the shipment window, and the quote can be written in one go.

If you are unsure which band fits your roast, ask for two samples, one Fine Cup and one Good Cup, and cup them side by side. The difference is easier to taste than to describe.
