A container of coffee, peanuts or sugar leaves Santos with a small file of paper — or, more and more, of PDFs — and your customs broker cannot release it at destination until that file is complete. Most delays at arrival are not about the cargo; they are about a document that is missing, late or wrong by one letter. Here is what should be in the set, and what each piece does.

## Commercial documents

**Commercial invoice.** Issued by the seller. It states the goods, the quantity, the unit and total price, the Incoterm and the payment terms, and it is the basis for customs valuation at destination. The description must match the contract, the packing list and the bill of lading word for word — a mismatch is the most common reason for a query at import.

**Packing list.** Also from the seller. It lists what is in each container: number of bags or pallets, net and gross weights, lot numbers, container and seal numbers. Customs and the surveyor at destination check the cargo against it.

**Contract or confirmation.** Not a shipping document, but keep it with the file: the bank will want it under a letter of credit, and it is the reference for any claim.

## Transport documents

**Bill of lading (B/L).** Issued by the shipping line once the container is on board. It is the receipt for the cargo, the evidence of the contract of carriage and — in its negotiable form — the title to the goods. Three ways to handle it: original B/Ls couriered to you or to your bank; a *telex release* (the line releases the cargo at destination without originals, once the seller surrenders them at origin); or a *sea waybill*, non-negotiable, for trusted counterparties. Under a letter of credit, the bank will usually require originals.

**Insurance certificate.** On CIF sales, issued by the seller's insurer in your favour. On FOB and CFR, you insure.

## Official certificates

**Certificate of origin.** Issued in Brazil by a chamber of commerce or trade association, it states that the goods are Brazilian. Some destinations use it for preferential tariffs — within MERCOSUR, for instance, or under Brazil's bilateral agreements — and then a specific form applies. Coffee also carries the ICO certificate of origin required by the International Coffee Organization.

**Phytosanitary certificate.** Issued by the Ministry of Agriculture (MAPA) after inspection, for products of plant origin: coffee, peanuts, Brazil nuts, macadamia, sesame, sugar, starch. It certifies that the lot is free of quarantine pests and, where the destination requires it, that the cargo was treated. Almonds from Portugal carry the equivalent certificate from the Portuguese authority.

**Health certificate.** For milk powder and other animal-origin products, issued by MAPA under the Federal Inspection Service (SIF), on the model the destination country has agreed with Brazil. The producing plant must be approved for that destination — which is why we confirm the approval before the contract, not after.

**Fumigation certificate.** Issued by the treatment company when the cargo or the container has been fumigated, stating the product, dose and exposure time. Required by many destinations for nuts and grains.

## Quality documents

**Certificate of analysis.** From the producer's laboratory or an independent one: aflatoxin for peanuts and Brazil nuts, moisture and defects for coffee, polarisation and colour for sugar, purity for sesame, composition and microbiology for milk powder and starch. It is issued per lot, and the buyer's specification decides which parameters it must show.

**Weight and quality certificate.** Optional, from an independent surveyor (SGS, Control Union and others) who samples and weighs the cargo at stuffing. Common on larger contracts and under letters of credit.

## When you should have them

Draft invoice and packing list before stuffing, so you can check the descriptions. Copies of the full set, including the B/L, within two or three working days of departure. Originals — when originals are required — by courier or through the bank in time to reach you before the vessel does. On a 15-day transit to Europe that is tight, which is why we prepare the certificates while the container is still being stuffed, and why a single team handling contract, logistics and paperwork is not a luxury but the reason the file arrives complete.
