Almost every quote for Brazilian food is written in one of three Incoterms — FOB, CFR or CIF — and the three letters decide who pays for what, and from which moment the cargo is your problem. The rules are set by the International Chamber of Commerce (Incoterms 2020). Here is what they mean when the port is Santos.

## FOB Santos — Free On Board

The seller delivers the goods **on board the vessel** at Santos, cleared for export. From that moment risk passes to you. You book the vessel, pay the ocean freight and arrange insurance, usually through your own forwarder. The seller pays everything before that point: inland haulage, terminal handling (THC), customs clearance, certificates and export documents.

FOB suits buyers who ship a lot from Brazil and have freight contracts of their own. It puts the ocean leg in your hands — which is an advantage if your rates are better than ours, and a coordination cost if they are not.

## CFR — Cost and Freight

Same delivery point, same risk transfer — on board at Santos — but the **seller books and pays the ocean freight** to the named destination port. Insurance stays with you. In practice CFR is the most common term for containerised food out of Brazil: you get one price that includes freight to your port, without taking on the freight negotiation, and you insure the cargo under your own open policy.

## CIF — Cost, Insurance and Freight

CFR plus **cargo insurance bought by the seller**, in your favour, covering the voyage. Under Incoterms 2020, CIF requires minimum cover (Institute Cargo Clauses C) for 110 % of the invoice value; wider cover can be agreed. CIF is the right term when you do not carry an open policy, when your bank asks for it under a letter of credit, or when you simply want one document set from one counterparty.

## What does not change

In all three terms the seller clears the goods for export, and risk passes when the cargo is on board at Santos. None of the three covers import duties, import clearance or delivery inland at destination — those are yours, or you would be asking for DAP or DDP, which few Brazilian food exporters offer.

A note the ICC itself makes: for containerised cargo, the formally correct terms are FCA, CPT and CIP, because the container is handed to the carrier at the terminal, not lifted over the ship's rail. The market keeps using FOB, CFR and CIF anyway. If your contract or your bank prefers the container terms, we quote them; the price is the same.

## Transit times from Santos

Approximate, port to port, on direct or single-transhipment services: 12 to 18 days to the US East Coast; 15 to 22 days to Northern Europe; 18 to 25 days to the Mediterranean; 30 to 35 days to the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea; 30 to 40 days to the Black Sea and the Baltic. Add a few days at each end for terminal cut-off and release. Coffee, nuts and powders are shipped in dry containers; peanuts on long routes travel better ventilated or in a reefer at a mild set point.

## Why our CFR price is a number

When the seller and the forwarder are two companies, a CFR quote is built on a freight estimate that may have changed by the time the booking is made. In our group the freight desk sits next to the trading desk: the rate on the quote is the rate on the booking, and if the line changes something, you hear it from the same person who sold you the lot. That is the practical difference of buying from a trading company that also carries the cargo.

Tell us the product, the volume and the destination port, and ask for the term you prefer. If you are unsure, ask for CFR: it is the simplest to compare between suppliers.
